# UX Canon 02 — Information Architecture, Navigation, and Flows

Domain: how the app is organized and how users move through it. Compiled 2026-07-22 from cross-source consensus: Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g), Baymard Institute, Apple Human Interface Guidelines (HIG), Material 3, GOV.UK Design System & Service Manual, Vercel Web Interface Guidelines, growth.design, and platform documentation. Each principle is tagged [mechanical] (checkable by rule) or [judgment] (requires a call), with sources.

---

## 1. Information architecture and hierarchy

1. **Structure the app around user tasks, not your org chart or database schema.** Users think in goals ("log a workout"), not in your internal modules; the IA must mirror their mental model, validated by card sorting or tree testing, not by how the code is organized. — [NN/g IA corpus; GOV.UK service manual] — [judgment]
2. **Prefer broad-and-shallow over narrow-and-deep hierarchies.** More options per level with fewer levels beats a deep tree; every extra level is a chance to get lost, and studies consistently show shallow trees are navigated faster and with fewer errors. — [NN/g "Flat vs. Deep Hierarchies"] — [judgment]
3. **Keep primary content within three taps/clicks of the entry point — but never sacrifice scent to hit a click count.** The "three-click rule" is folklore as a hard limit; the real consensus is that each click must feel like progress. Depth is fine if every step is obviously right. — [NN/g "3-Click Rule"; Baymard] — [judgment]
4. **Every item lives in exactly one obvious primary place; cross-link secondary access rather than duplicating.** Duplicated destinations make users doubt whether two entries are the same thing. One canonical home, plus contextual links to it. — [NN/g IA guidelines] — [judgment]
5. **Group by meaning to the user, not by feature age or team ownership.** "New stuff" sections, or splitting one concept across two menus because two teams built it, are IA defects users notice instantly. — [NN/g; GOV.UK] — [judgment]
6. **Categories must be mutually exclusive at the top level.** When a user could reasonably file an item under two top-level categories, they will guess wrong half the time; either merge, rename, or cross-link explicitly. — [NN/g IA study guide] — [judgment]
7. **Match menu size to memory: 5–8 top-level destinations is the practical ceiling for app navigation.** Beyond that, comprehension and scanning collapse; regroup or demote. (Websites can carry more in visible nav bars; apps cannot.) — [Material 3 navigation bar: 3–5 destinations; Apple HIG tab bars; NN/g menu design] — [mechanical]
8. **Expose the structure — never make users build the map from scratch.** Visible navigation, section landing pages, and consistent placement teach the shape of the app; hidden or fully search-dependent structure forces users to hold the map in their head. — [NN/g navigation visibility research] — [judgment]
9. **Test the IA with tree tests / card sorts before shipping big reorganizations.** Findability is empirical; internal stakeholders are the worst possible test subjects because they already know the structure. — [NN/g; GOV.UK service manual] — [mechanical]
10. **Design the IA for the 80% task first; put the long tail behind "More", search, or settings.** Frequency of use, not completeness, determines what gets top-level real estate. — [Apple HIG; NN/g] — [judgment]
11. **When the app grows, restructure — don't append.** Bolting each new feature onto the nav one at a time produces the classic "junk drawer" IA; periodic re-architecture is expected maintenance. — [NN/g navigation debt discussions] — [judgment]
12. **Home must orient, not just decorate.** The start screen answers "what is this app, what can I do, what needs my attention now" — it is the top of the hierarchy, and every top-level section should be reachable from it. — [NN/g homepage guidelines; Apple HIG] — [judgment]

## 2. Navigation patterns — which one, when, and its rules

13. **Mobile apps with 3–5 coequal top-level destinations use a bottom tab bar.** This is the dominant, expected pattern on both platforms; it keeps navigation persistent, visible, and thumb-reachable. — [Apple HIG tab bars; Material 3 navigation bar; NN/g mobile navigation primer] — [mechanical]
14. **Tabs are persistent: the tab bar stays visible across the app's main screens and never disappears mid-hierarchy without reason.** Users rely on it as the constant "you can always get back" layer; hiding it (except in focused/fullscreen modes like players or editors) breaks the safety net. — [Apple HIG; Material 3] — [mechanical]
15. **Tapping the current tab again returns to that tab's root (and/or scrolls to top).** This is a platform-level convention users depend on to escape deep stacks. — [Apple HIG; Material 3; observed universal in best-in-class apps] — [mechanical]
16. **Each tab keeps its own navigation stack; switching tabs preserves where you were in the other tab.** Switching to Profile and back to Feed must not reset Feed to its root. — [Apple HIG; Material navigation state guidance] — [mechanical]
17. **Never use a tab as an action button.** Tabs navigate to destinations; a "+" that opens a modal composer belongs as a floating/central action treated distinctly, not as a peer destination that leaves no screen behind. — [Apple HIG; Material 3 FAB vs nav bar] — [mechanical]
18. **Tab icons get labels.** Icon-only tab bars fail recognition tests except for a handful of universal glyphs; every destination shows a short text label at all times. — [NN/g icon usability; Material 3; Apple HIG] — [mechanical]
19. **Don't hide primary navigation behind a hamburger when you have the space to show it.** Hidden navigation cuts discoverability roughly in half and increases task time and perceived difficulty; hamburgers are a compromise for overflow/secondary items, not a home for primary destinations. — [NN/g hamburger research; Baymard] — [mechanical]
20. **On desktop widths, expose navigation as a visible top bar or side rail — never a mobile hamburger stretched to desktop.** Screen space exists; use it. Responsive apps commonly graduate: bottom tabs (phone) → navigation rail (tablet) → drawer/sidebar (desktop). — [NN/g; Material 3 canonical layouts] — [mechanical]
21. **Hub-and-spoke fits tool collections with independent workspaces; use it only when spokes genuinely don't interconnect.** (e.g., a settings app, or a suite launcher.) If users frequently move between spokes, hub-and-spoke forces constant round-trips through the hub — use tabs or a rail instead. — [NN/g mobile navigation patterns] — [judgment]
22. **Sequential (wizard) navigation is for linear one-off tasks, not for browsing structures.** Forcing a step order onto content people want to jump around defeats them; conversely, letting people wander in a flow that has hard dependencies produces broken states. — [NN/g wizards; GOV.UK one-thing-per-page] — [judgment]
23. **Use a navigation drawer only for secondary/overflow destinations or very large numbers of destinations — and accept the discoverability cost knowingly.** Browse-mostly products and power-user sections tolerate drawers; core daily-use destinations don't. — [Material 3 drawer guidance; NN/g] — [judgment]
24. **Segmented controls / in-page tabs subdivide one screen's content; they never replace app-level navigation.** Two levels of tab-like controls on one screen must be visually distinct or users conflate them. — [Apple HIG segmented controls; Material 3 tabs] — [mechanical]
25. **Keep navigation placement, order, and contents identical on every screen where it appears.** Reordering or conditionally removing items as the user moves around destroys spatial memory. (Adaptive/personalized reordering of primary nav is an anti-pattern.) — [NN/g consistency heuristic; Material 3] — [mechanical]
26. **Indicate the current location in the navigation itself.** The active tab/section is visibly selected on every screen; a nav bar that doesn't show "you are here" is a map without a pin. — [NN/g wayfinding; Material 3 active indicator; WCAG-adjacent] — [mechanical]
27. **Primary actions and primary navigation live in the thumb zone on phones.** Bottom-anchored navigation and key actions; top corners are the hardest reach on large phones. — [Apple HIG; Material ergonomics; Baymard mobile] — [mechanical]
28. **Gestural navigation supplements, never replaces, visible controls.** Swipe-to-go-back, swipe between tabs etc. are accelerators; every gesture-reachable destination also has a visible on-screen affordance. — [Apple HIG gestures; NN/g invisible UI risk] — [mechanical]
29. **Don't invent a novel navigation scheme when a standard one fits.** Users import navigation habits from every other app they use; novelty here is a tax paid on every single session. — [NN/g Jakob's Law; CLAUDE.md §5 aligns] — [judgment]
30. **Provide an escape from every screen.** No dead ends: every screen has visible back/close/cancel; modal surfaces always show an explicit dismiss affordance in addition to any gesture. — [NN/g "user control and freedom"; Apple HIG modality] — [mechanical]

## 3. Labeling and wayfinding

31. **Navigation labels are front-loaded, specific nouns users would say themselves.** "Workouts", "History", "Profile" — not clever brand words, not internal jargon, not "Stuff" or "Zone". If a first-time user can't predict what's behind the label, the label failed. — [NN/g link labels; GOV.UK content design] — [judgment]
32. **Never use the same label for two different destinations, or two labels for the same destination.** One name per concept, everywhere it appears: nav item, page title, link text, and breadcrumb all match. — [NN/g consistency; GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
33. **The screen title matches the label the user tapped to get there.** Tapping "Settings" must land on a screen titled "Settings"; mismatches make users wonder whether they arrived at the right place. — [NN/g scent of information; GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
34. **Avoid vague catch-alls ("More", "Other", "Tools") for anything users must find.** A junk-drawer label is where findability goes to die; it's acceptable only for genuinely rare, low-stakes items. — [NN/g menu labels] — [judgment]
35. **Icons never carry meaning alone.** Except for a tiny set of near-universal symbols (search, home, settings-gear, back arrow), every icon that navigates gets a text label; icon-only navigation reliably fails testing. — [NN/g icon usability] — [mechanical]
36. **Every screen answers three wayfinding questions at a glance: Where am I? What's here? Where can I go?** Title, content preview, and visible navigation respectively; a screen missing any of the three disorients. — [NN/g wayfinding framework] — [judgment]
37. **Show breadcrumbs on hierarchies deeper than two levels (web/desktop).** Breadcrumbs cost one line and provide location, one-tap ancestor access, and structure teaching; the current page is the last, non-linked crumb. — [NN/g breadcrumb guidelines; Baymard] — [mechanical]
38. **Breadcrumbs reflect the hierarchy, not the user's visit history.** They answer "where is this in the structure," not "how did I get here" — the back button owns history. — [NN/g breadcrumbs] — [mechanical]
39. **Highlight state changes in place: after a filter, sort, or context switch, the screen visibly confirms the new state.** Active filter chips, result counts, selected-state styling — the user should never have to remember what they set. — [NN/g visibility of system status; Baymard filtering] — [mechanical]
40. **Empty sections still orient.** An empty state names the section, says why it's empty, and offers the next action — it never renders as a blank page that looks broken. — [NN/g empty states; Material 3] — [mechanical]
41. **Use the user's language for section names and confirm with real users, not the team.** Internal names leak ("Runner", "Composer", "Entities") and are meaningless outside the building. — [NN/g match between system and real world] — [judgment]

## 4. Back behavior and history

42. **Browser back must always work and always go where the user expects.** Breaking, hijacking, or ignoring the back button is one of the most-hated defects on the web; back is the second-most-used navigation action after links. — [NN/g back button research; Baymard] — [mechanical]
43. **Every distinct view the user perceives as "a new place" gets a history entry; every transient state does not.** Full navigations, opened detail views, and (on mobile web) opened overlays that fill the screen push history; hover states, accordion toggles, and small popovers don't. — [Baymard mobile web overlays; Vercel Web Interface Guidelines] — [judgment]
44. **Back never loses work.** If going back would discard meaningful unsaved input, either persist it (restore on return) or intercept with a clear confirm — silent destruction is forbidden; so is confirming when nothing would be lost. — [NN/g error prevention; Apple HIG; GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
45. **Back restores prior state — scroll position, filters, entered data — not just the prior URL.** Returning to a list must land where the user left, not at the top with filters reset ("back-to-listing position loss" is a top documented e-commerce failure). — [Baymard product list & back behavior; NN/g] — [mechanical]
46. **In-app back (the ← in your header) navigates up/back within the app; it must not exit the app or jump to unrelated screens.** On Android, system back and in-app back should agree; diverging them confuses everyone. — [Material back-handling guidance; Apple HIG navigation bars] — [mechanical]
47. **Distinguish "back" (history) from "up" (hierarchy) and make deep-linked entries sane.** A user who deep-links into a detail screen still needs an "up" path to the parent list even though they have no in-app history. — [Material navigation principles; Android back-stack synthesis] — [mechanical]
48. **Closing a modal, sheet, or overlay returns exactly to the underlying context, unchanged.** No scroll jumps, no reloaded data, no lost selection beneath a dismissed layer. — [Apple HIG modality; NN/g overlays] — [mechanical]
49. **Never trap users with history spam.** Redirect chains and interstitials must not stuff history so that "back" bounces the user forward again; replace, don't push, for intermediate redirects. — [Vercel WIG; long-standing web consensus] — [mechanical]
50. **Multi-step flows define back per step deliberately: back goes to the previous step with its data intact, and post-completion back must not resubmit or duplicate.** Use redirect-after-POST (or equivalent) so back after success shows a safe screen, not a "resubmit form?" dialog. — [GOV.UK patterns; Baymard checkout; PRG pattern consensus] — [mechanical]
51. **Support platform back gestures (edge-swipe iOS, back-swipe/button Android) everywhere, including custom views.** A screen that ignores the platform back gesture feels broken on that platform. — [Apple HIG; Android predictive back] — [mechanical]

## 5. URL and deep-link state

52. **Every screen a user can perceive as a place has a stable, shareable URL (web) or deep link (app).** If it can be seen, it can be linked, bookmarked, and reopened. — [Vercel WIG; NN/g; universal web architecture consensus] — [mechanical]
53. **Reflect view-defining UI state in the URL: active tab, filters, sort, search query, pagination, selected item, expanded panel.** The test: refresh must restore exactly what the user saw, and a shared link must show the recipient the same view. Anything held only in component state fails both. — [Vercel WIG "deep-link everything"; NN/g] — [mechanical]
54. **Don't put transient or private state in the URL.** Scroll position (restore it via browser mechanisms instead), form field contents, session tokens, one-time flags — none of these belong in a shareable string. — [Vercel WIG; web security consensus] — [mechanical]
55. **Filters and sorts update the URL via replace (not push) while the user is refining; discrete navigations push.** Twenty filter tweaks should not require twenty presses of back to escape. — [Vercel WIG; Baymard faceted navigation] — [judgment]
56. **URLs are readable and guessable: hierarchical paths for hierarchy, query params for view state.** `/workouts/legs?sort=recent` beats `/p?id=93124&m=2`; humans read URLs as a secondary breadcrumb. — [NN/g URL usability; GOV.UK] — [judgment]
57. **Deep links must survive auth: an unauthenticated user hitting a protected link logs in and lands on the originally requested screen, not the home page.** Losing the destination after login is a canonical, universally condemned defect. — [Baymard accounts; Auth0/industry consensus; NN/g] — [mechanical]
58. **Deep links into the app construct proper surrounding context.** Arriving at a detail screen from a notification or shared link yields working up-navigation and correct tab selection, as if the user had browsed there. — [Android deep-link back-stack guidance; Apple HIG universal links] — [mechanical]
59. **Old URLs never die silently.** Renamed or restructured routes redirect to their successors; removed content gets a helpful, navigable 404 that offers search and top destinations — never a dead-end error. — [GOV.UK; NN/g 404 guidelines; "cool URIs don't change" (W3C)] — [mechanical]
60. **Navigation uses real links.** Anything that navigates renders as `<a>`/platform link so open-in-new-tab, copy-link, middle-click, and long-press work; `<div onClick>` navigation breaks the web's contract. — [Vercel WIG; NN/g] — [mechanical]
61. **Shared/deep-linked views degrade gracefully when the target is gone or inaccessible.** "This workout was deleted" with a path onward, not a crash or a spinner forever. — [platform deep-link guidance; NN/g error pages] — [mechanical]

## 6. Search

62. **Any app whose content outgrows its navigation gets search — and search is a supplement to good IA, never a substitute for it.** Users who fail navigation fall back to search; if both fail, they leave. — [NN/g search corpus; Baymard] — [judgment]
63. **Put search where users look for it: top of screen, recognizable field or magnifier icon, reachable from every screen where finding things is a plausible goal.** Burying search inside a menu suppresses its use. — [NN/g search placement; Baymard] — [mechanical]
64. **State the scope.** The field (placeholder or label) says what is being searched — "Search exercises" vs. a bare "Search" — and scoped search offers an obvious way to widen to everything. — [NN/g scoped search; Baymard] — [mechanical]
65. **Support recents and suggestions.** Recent queries on focus, and as-you-type suggestions once typing starts, dramatically cut effort and typos — expected behavior in 2026, especially on mobile. — [NN/g search suggestions; Baymard autocomplete] — [mechanical]
66. **Autocomplete suggests queries and top results, is keyboard-navigable, and never traps focus.** Highlight the matching portion; cap the list (~10); Escape/tap-away dismisses cleanly. — [Baymard autocomplete guidelines; NN/g] — [mechanical]
67. **Tolerate human input: typos, synonyms, singular/plural, diacritics, and common alternate names all return the intended results.** "Zero results because you typed 'dumbell'" is a defect, not user error. — [Baymard search query types; NN/g] — [mechanical]
68. **The results page shows the query (editable, still in the field), the result count, and the scope.** Users iterate on searches; forcing them to retype from scratch punishes iteration. — [NN/g search results; Baymard] — [mechanical]
69. **No-results pages are recovery tools, never dead ends.** Say clearly that nothing matched *what*, then offer: spelling suggestions, loosened filters, related categories, and popular content. An empty white page with "0 results" is a canonical failure. — [NN/g no-results; Baymard] — [mechanical]
70. **Keep active filters visible and individually removable on results, with one control to clear all.** Users forget applied filters and then mistrust "missing" results. — [Baymard faceted search; NN/g filters] — [mechanical]
71. **Search state is shareable and restorable.** Query, filters, and sort live in the URL (see §5); back from a result returns to the same result list at the same position. — [Vercel WIG; Baymard] — [mechanical]
72. **Rank by usefulness, not recency or alphabet, and show why a result matched when it isn't obvious.** Snippets/highlighted terms let users judge results without opening each one. — [NN/g search results relevance] — [judgment]

## 7. Onboarding and first-run

73. **Minimize time-to-first-value: the first session must deliver the app's core value, not a tour of it.** Every screen between install and value bleeds users; design first-run backwards from "the moment it was worth it." — [growth.design; NN/g onboarding; Apple HIG onboarding] — [judgment]
74. **Prefer progressive, contextual onboarding over front-loaded carousels and forced tours.** Teach a feature at the moment it's first relevant; multi-screen intro slideshows are skipped, forgotten, and resented. Deck-of-cards intros survive only for setting one-time expectations, and even then must be skippable. — [NN/g mobile onboarding; growth.design; Material guidance] — [judgment]
75. **Every onboarding step is skippable, and skipping is safe.** "Skip" is visible (not hidden in tiny gray text), and skipping never leaves the user in a broken half-configured state. — [Apple HIG; NN/g] — [mechanical]
76. **Let people try before signing up wherever the product allows.** Demand an account only when it's genuinely required to deliver value (saving data, sync); gating the very first look behind registration is a documented abandonment driver. — [NN/g login walls; Baymard; growth.design] — [judgment]
77. **Ask personalization questions only when the answer visibly changes the experience, and say so.** Each question in a first-run quiz must earn its place ("we'll use this to size your plan"); interrogation-style onboarding with no visible payoff is a top uninstall trigger. — [growth.design case studies; NN/g] — [judgment]
78. **Defer every deferrable ask.** Notifications permission, contacts, ratings, upsells — none of these belong in the first minute (see §13). First-run has exactly one job: deliver value. — [Apple HIG; growth.design; NN/g] — [judgment]
79. **Design the empty first-day experience deliberately.** Before the user has data, every screen they can reach shows a purposeful empty state that teaches and prompts the first action — sample data or templates where appropriate. — [NN/g empty states; Material] — [judgment]
80. **Use action-driven teaching: have the user do the core thing once, with guidance, rather than watch it described.** Completing one real task teaches more than any tour and creates investment. — [growth.design; product onboarding consensus] — [judgment]
81. **Never re-onboard a returning user.** Seen-it state persists across sessions and devices; showing the intro carousel again after reinstall/login is sloppy but showing it again every session is a defect. — [platform consensus] — [mechanical]
82. **Tooltips and coach marks: one at a time, anchored to the real control, dismiss on interaction, never stacked into a tour of five.** Simultaneous or chained overlays get dismissed blind. — [NN/g instructional overlays; Material] — [mechanical]
83. **Instrument the funnel: know where first-run users drop and treat those steps as defects.** Onboarding is the highest-leverage funnel in the product; unmeasured onboarding cannot be improved. — [growth.design; universal PLG consensus] — [mechanical]

## 8. Multi-step flows and wizards

84. **Break long tasks into focused steps — one decision or topic per screen — rather than one giant form.** GOV.UK's "one thing per page" is the strong form; the consensus core is that each step should have a single clear job, which especially matters on phones. — [GOV.UK design system; NN/g wizards; Baymard checkout] — [judgment]
85. **Show progress: where the user is, how much remains.** Step indicators ("Step 2 of 4") or a progress bar for linear flows; users abandon flows whose length they can't estimate. Don't show a step tracker for 2-step flows — it adds noise. — [NN/g wizards; Baymard checkout; GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
86. **Order steps from easy to invested.** Start with low-friction, engaging steps; ask for the heavy commitment (payment, personal data) after the user has built momentum and seen the summary of what they're getting. — [Baymard checkout sequence; behavioral consensus] — [judgment]
87. **Every step allows going back without losing data — forward data too.** Values entered on step 3 survive a trip back to step 1 and forward again. — [NN/g wizards; GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
88. **Long or high-stakes flows save progress and support resume.** Explicit draft saving or silent auto-persistence, with a clear re-entry point ("Continue where you left off"); a crash, timeout, or accidental close must not restart the flow at zero. — [GOV.UK "check your answers"/save-and-return patterns; Baymard] — [mechanical]
89. **End with a review step before irreversible submission.** Show everything the user entered, grouped, with per-item "Change" links that return to that step and then bring the user back to review — not force a re-walk of the whole flow. — [GOV.UK "Check your answers"; Baymard order review] — [mechanical]
90. **After submission, confirm explicitly: what happened, what happens next, reference/receipt, and a clear onward path.** The confirmation screen is part of the flow, not an afterthought; leave no doubt the action succeeded. — [GOV.UK confirmation pattern; Baymard] — [mechanical]
91. **Validate per step, at the moment the user finishes the field or step — not only at the end.** Late-bound rejection of step-1 input at step 5 is a canonical rage pattern; equally, don't scream errors while the user is still typing. — [NN/g form validation timing; Baymard] — [mechanical]
92. **Don't ask for information you don't need, already have, or could derive.** Every field must justify itself; prefill everything known (and let the user correct it). Baymard: typical checkouts carry ~2x the necessary fields. — [GOV.UK; Baymard form fields] — [judgment]
93. **Only make a flow a modal wizard if completing it is short and atomic; long flows get full screens with real URLs per step.** Multi-step forms trapped inside a dialog fight back behavior, resume, and links. — [NN/g modality; GOV.UK; Vercel WIG] — [judgment]
94. **Branch the flow instead of showing irrelevant steps.** Use earlier answers to skip inapplicable questions; making everyone walk every branch is why long forms feel bureaucratic. — [GOV.UK question protocols] — [judgment]
95. **Keep the primary "continue" action visually dominant, consistent in position and label across steps, and disable-free.** Prefer enabled buttons that produce clear errors over mysteriously disabled ones. — [NN/g disabled buttons; GOV.UK button guidance] — [mechanical]

## 9. Progressive disclosure

96. **Show the common case first; reveal advanced or rare options on demand.** Defaults up front, "Advanced"/"More options" behind one deliberate interaction — this cuts error rates and learning time without capping power. — [NN/g progressive disclosure (canonical article)] — [judgment]
97. **What's hidden must be findable: the reveal control is visible, labeled, and hints at what it hides.** Progressive disclosure fails when the trigger itself is invisible — that's just hidden functionality. — [NN/g; Material expansion patterns] — [mechanical]
98. **Disclosure has levels, not mazes: at most one or two layers deep.** If users must expand-inside-expand-inside-expand, the categorization is wrong. — [NN/g progressive disclosure] — [judgment]
99. **Staged disclosure for flows, progressive disclosure for options.** Sequencing complexity across wizard steps (staged) and hiding advanced settings behind a toggle (progressive) are different tools; use each where it fits. — [NN/g] — [judgment]
100. **Never disclose punitively: information needed to make the current decision is never hidden behind an extra tap.** Price, consequences, and requirements must be visible before commitment (see §12). — [Baymard; FTC/dark-pattern consensus] — [mechanical]
101. **Remember disclosure state where it helps, reset it where it doesn't.** Power users who always open "Advanced" shouldn't reopen it every session; transient expansions in lists generally reset. — [platform consensus] — [judgment]

## 10. Settings organization

102. **Settings are grouped into a small number of labeled categories users can predict: Account, Notifications, Appearance/Preferences, Privacy & Security, Support/About.** These groupings are near-universal across best-in-class apps; inventing exotic categories forces guessing. — [Apple HIG settings; Material; NN/g] — [judgment]
103. **Order groups by frequency-of-need, not alphabetically or by build order.** The things people actually change (notifications, profile) go up top; About/legal goes last. — [Apple HIG; platform consensus] — [judgment]
104. **Make settings searchable once they exceed roughly two screens.** Settings search is standard in every major OS and large app; hunting through nested categories is the alternative. — [Apple/Android system settings precedent; NN/g] — [mechanical]
105. **Each setting states its effect in plain language, shows its current value at the group level where possible, and applies immediately (or says exactly when it will).** "Changes take effect after restart" must be said at the moment of change. — [Apple HIG; GOV.UK content style] — [mechanical]
106. **Separate the danger zone.** Destructive account actions — delete account, erase data, reset — live in their own clearly separated section, styled as destructive, with typed/explicit confirmation, never adjacent to routine items. — [GitHub/industry "danger zone" convention; NN/g error prevention] — [mechanical]
107. **Account deletion must exist, be findable in-app, and actually delete.** Both app stores mandate in-app account deletion for apps with account creation; hiding it behind support email is both a policy violation and a dark pattern. — [Apple App Store & Google Play policies; FTC consensus] — [mechanical]
108. **Settings changes never require the user to know a "save" existed.** Either auto-save each toggle (mobile norm) or an unmissable save bar with unsaved-changes protection (desktop/web form-style settings) — never silent discard on navigate-away. — [Material; NN/g unsaved changes] — [mechanical]
109. **Don't bury product features in settings, and don't leak settings into the product.** Settings hold preferences (how the app behaves); actions and content belong in the app proper. A feature nobody finds because it's a settings toggle is a placement defect. — [Apple HIG; NN/g] — [judgment]
110. **Every notification and email the app sends is individually controllable from one notifications settings screen** (see §14). — [Apple HIG; Material; CAN-SPAM/GDPR-adjacent consensus] — [mechanical]

## 11. Account and auth flows

111. **Signup asks for the minimum: typically email + password, or a single social/passkey tap.** Every extra signup field is measurable abandonment; profile completion comes later, in context. — [Baymard accounts; NN/g; growth.design] — [mechanical]
112. **Offer sign-in with the platform's expected accelerators — Apple/Google/passkeys — alongside email, and keep the option list short.** A wall of eight identity providers is choice paralysis; 2–3 plus email is the norm. Apple requires "Sign in with Apple" parity when other social logins are offered. — [Apple policy; industry consensus; Baymard] — [mechanical]
113. **Show password rules before the user types, validate them live as checkmarks, and allow paste and reveal.** Discovering the rules through rejection, blocking paste, or permanently masked input are all documented defects; masking with an optional reveal toggle is the standard. — [NN/g password UX; NIST 800-63B; Baymard] — [mechanical]
114. **Distinguish login from signup unmistakably, and catch the crossover errors.** "This email already has an account — log in instead?" on signup, and a clear "no account found — create one?" on login; silently failing either way strands users. (This repo has a known duplicate-email defect of exactly this class.) — [Baymard accounts; NN/g] — [mechanical]
115. **Password reset is a first-class flow: one obvious link at login, email arrives fast, link works once, expiry is stated, and success logs the user in (or lands at login prefilled).** Reset is used constantly; treating it as an edge case punishes your most frustrated users. — [Baymard; NN/g; NIST] — [mechanical]
116. **Errors on login never reveal which part was wrong beyond necessity, but always say what to do next.** "Email or password is incorrect — reset password?" balances security and recovery. — [OWASP; NN/g error messages] — [mechanical]
117. **Keep users logged in on their own devices by default; re-authenticate for sensitive actions instead of expiring cheap sessions aggressively.** Frequent forced re-login is a top mobile complaint; step-up auth (confirm password/biometric for payment or deletion) provides the security without the daily tax. — [NIST 800-63B; Apple HIG; industry consensus] — [judgment]
118. **When a session does expire mid-task, preserve the work and return the user to it after re-auth.** Expiry that discards a half-written entry is data loss (see §16); the re-auth screen says why it appeared. — [NN/g; GOV.UK session timeout patterns] — [mechanical]
119. **Support biometric unlock where the platform offers it, as an opt-in accelerator over — not a replacement for — the account credential.** — [Apple HIG; Android identity guidance] — [mechanical]
120. **Logout is findable (profile/settings, standard placement), immediate, confirmed only if there is unsaved work, and scopes clearly (this device vs. all devices where relevant).** — [platform consensus; Baymard] — [mechanical]
121. **Email verification never blocks first value unless the product truly requires it.** Let the user in, banner the unverified state, gate only what must be gated. — [growth.design; industry consensus] — [judgment]

## 12. Subscription, paywall, and checkout flows — the ethical consensus

122. **Show the full price, billing period, and renewal terms before any commitment control.** What it costs, how often, when the first charge happens, and what happens after any trial — on the paywall itself, not behind a link. This is both consensus UX and (post-2024 FTC/EU rules) law in major markets. — [FTC Negative Option rule; Baymard; App Store policies] — [mechanical]
123. **Free trials state their length, what happens at the end, and when the user will be charged — and the user is notified before conversion.** "Trial ends June 3; we'll remind you 2 days before" is the trust-building standard. — [App store subscription policies; FTC; growth.design] — [mechanical]
124. **Cancellation is as easy as signup and in the same channel.** Online signup ⇒ online cancel, findable from subscription settings in ≤2 steps; requiring phone calls, chat, or "retention specialists" is a legally-targeted dark pattern. Post-cancel: confirm, state the end date, and say what happens to the user's data. — [FTC click-to-cancel; EU consumer law; universal ethical consensus] — [mechanical]
125. **One save-attempt maximum on cancel, honestly framed.** A single "here's what you'll lose / here's a cheaper plan" screen with a clearly equal "Continue canceling" is tolerated; chained guilt screens, hidden confirm buttons, and confirm-shaming copy ("No, I hate saving money") are condemned across every source. — [FTC dark-pattern guidance; NN/g; growth.design] — [mechanical]
126. **Pricing pages compare plans in a scannable table with honest anchoring: real differences, a recommended tier at most highlighted, no fake "most popular" pressure or hidden fees.** Annual-vs-monthly toggles show real per-period totals, not just discounted per-month math. — [Baymard pricing; NN/g comparison tables; dark-patterns consensus] — [judgment]
127. **The paywall states what's free vs. paid truthfully and never appears mid-task as a trap.** Letting a user build something and then holding it hostage behind a surprise upgrade is a dark pattern; gate at entry to the premium capability, before invested work. — [growth.design; consensus] — [judgment]
128. **Checkout: offer guest checkout prominently; never force account creation to buy.** ~19% of shoppers abandon over forced registration; the winning pattern is guest checkout, then a post-purchase one-tap "save your details as an account." — [Baymard checkout (canonical); NN/g] — [mechanical]
129. **Enclose checkout: strip distracting navigation during payment, but keep an obvious exit and the cart/summary always visible.** A persistent order summary (items, costs, shipping, taxes, total) at every step; totals never change silently. — [Baymard checkout; NN/g] — [mechanical]
130. **Surprise costs are the #1 abandonment cause: show shipping, taxes, and fees as early as estimable — never for the first time at the final step.** — [Baymard cart abandonment research] — [mechanical]
131. **Support platform payment accelerators (Apple Pay / Google Pay / saved cards) as top options, with card entry meeting all form conventions** (numeric keyboards, autofill, real-time formatting, clear error location). — [Baymard payment step; platform guidance] — [mechanical]
132. **No pre-checked add-ons, no sneaked items in the cart, no countdown-pressure timers unless the deadline is real.** Every one of these is on the named FTC/EU dark-pattern lists and in every reputable design source's condemned list. — [FTC; EU Digital Fairness work; NN/g deceptive patterns] — [mechanical]
133. **Refunds, plan changes, and pauses are self-serve where the market expects it, and pausing is offered where it genuinely fits the product** (a legitimate retention tool, unlike obstruction). — [industry consensus; growth.design] — [judgment]

## 13. Permission requests

134. **Never ask for permissions at launch, en masse.** The install-time or first-launch permission barrage is the single most condemned permission pattern; each request comes separately, at the moment of need. — [Apple HIG; Android permission guidance; NN/g] — [mechanical]
135. **Ask in context, triggered by the user's own action.** The user taps "Find gyms near me" → then the location request appears; permission grant rates are dramatically higher when the user just expressed the intent the permission serves. — [Apple HIG; Android; growth.design] — [mechanical]
136. **Prime before the one-shot system prompt — and only fire the native prompt after a yes.** A pre-permission screen in your own UI explains the benefit in one sentence; if the user declines your screen, the system prompt (which you often only get once) is preserved for a better moment. Don't prime for trivial, obviously-contextual asks. — [growth.design; Appcues/industry consensus; Apple HIG "make the case first"] — [judgment]
137. **Explain benefit, not mechanism, in the request copy.** "So Chad can track your runs" not "This app requires access to Location Services"; the purpose string is user-facing copy, not a legal notice. — [Apple HIG purpose strings; Android rationale API] — [judgment]
138. **Request the minimum scope and escalate later.** While-using before always-on location; approximate before precise; limited photo access before full library. Over-asking reads as creepy and triggers denials. — [Apple/Android permission models; privacy-by-design consensus] — [mechanical]
139. **Denial is a supported path, never a wall.** The feature degrades gracefully (manual entry, reduced mode), the app never nags in a loop, and re-requesting waits for a genuinely new context. — [Apple HIG; Android "don't repeat-prompt"; NN/g] — [mechanical]
140. **When a previously-denied permission is needed, explain and deep-link to system settings — since the OS won't re-prompt.** "Notifications are off for Chad. Turn them on in Settings → [button]" — with the app handling the return gracefully. — [Apple/Android platform behavior; consensus] — [mechanical]
141. **Never block the whole app on a non-essential permission.** Permission walls for nice-to-haves (contacts, notifications) are a documented uninstall trigger; only truly core permissions (camera for a camera app) may gate. — [Apple review guidelines; consensus] — [mechanical]

## 14. Notifications strategy

142. **Every notification must be worth an interruption from the user's point of view.** The bar is user value (their event, their reminder they asked for), not business value; "we miss you" and disguised ads burn the channel and drive app deletion — notification annoyance is a top-3 uninstall reason across studies. — [NN/g push notifications; Apple HIG; growth.design] — [judgment]
143. **Ask for notification permission only after demonstrating what notifications are for — never on first launch.** The user should already want the thing the notification delivers (e.g., after scheduling their first workout: "Want a reminder?"). — [see §13; Apple HIG; growth.design] — [mechanical]
144. **Offer per-category controls, not one master switch.** Users want workout reminders but not marketing; a single on/off forces all-or-nothing and loses the valuable channel with the junk. Categories map to Android channels / iOS notification settings. — [Android notification channels (mandatory); Apple HIG; NN/g] — [mechanical]
145. **Batch and digest low-urgency items.** Ten individual "someone liked your post" pushes become one summary; frequency caps and quiet hours are the operator's responsibility, not the user's. — [NN/g; platform summary/digest features; consensus] — [judgment]
146. **Every notification deep-links to the exact relevant screen, with correct back/up context.** Landing on the generic home screen after tapping a specific notification is a canonical defect. — [Apple HIG; Android; see §5] — [mechanical]
147. **Notifications are truthful and self-contained: the payload says what happened; no bait ("Someone viewed your profile! → paywall"), no fake urgency, no empty pings to drive opens.** — [dark-pattern consensus; platform policies] — [mechanical]
148. **In-app state and notifications stay consistent.** Acting on the item from the app clears the corresponding notification and badge; stale badges that don't correspond to anything actionable train users to ignore them. — [Apple HIG badging; consensus] — [mechanical]
149. **Opt-out is one tap from the message itself where the channel allows** (email unsubscribe link that works instantly; long-press channel controls on Android; in-app "stop these" affordances). Making users hunt settings to stop a notification type they're staring at is friction in the wrong place. — [CAN-SPAM/GDPR for email; Android; NN/g] — [mechanical]
150. **Choose the channel by urgency and locus: push for time-sensitive user events, in-app inbox for non-urgent history, email for records and long-form, badges only for actionable counts.** Duplicating every message on every channel is spam by architecture. — [NN/g omnichannel; consensus] — [judgment]

## 15. Cross-device and multi-tab continuity

151. **A signed-in user's data is current on every device without manual sync.** Log a workout on the phone; it's on the laptop when they open it. Server-side state with background refresh is table stakes for account-based apps in 2026. — [Apple HIG (Handoff-era expectation); industry consensus] — [mechanical]
152. **Sync conflicts resolve without data loss, silently when safe and explicitly when not.** Last-write-wins for trivial prefs; for meaningful content, preserve both/merge or ask — never silently discard one side's edits. — [consensus across sync-platform guidance] — [judgment]
153. **In-progress state (drafts, carts, half-finished flows) syncs, not just committed data — where the product plausibly spans devices.** Starting on one device and finishing on another is normal behavior, and the cart that exists only on the phone loses sales. — [Baymard cross-device cart persistence; consensus] — [judgment]
154. **The web app behaves in multiple tabs.** Two tabs don't corrupt each other's state; auth changes (logout, login-as-other) in one tab are detected in others rather than letting a stale-session tab silently fail or cross accounts. — [Vercel WIG-adjacent; web consensus] — [mechanical]
155. **Responsive is one product, not two.** The same features, same data, and same mental model at every width; "use our app for that" walls on mobile web, or desktop-only features with no path, are documented user-hostile patterns. — [NN/g responsive; Baymard mobile web] — [judgment]
156. **Show sync status only when it's news.** Quiet when everything is normal; visible and specific when offline, syncing large changes, or failed — with retry. A permanently spinning sync icon is noise; a silent failure is data loss. — [Material offline patterns; consensus] — [mechanical]
157. **Deep links, emails, and notifications work regardless of which device opens them** — routing to the app when installed, to an equivalent web view when not (universal links / app links, with sane web fallbacks). — [Apple universal links; Android app links] — [mechanical]

## 16. Interruptions and resumability

158. **Assume every session ends mid-task — and make that free.** Phone calls, app switches, tab closes, battery death: the app continuously persists meaningful input so that no plausible interruption costs the user work. Autosave is the default posture in 2026; explicit "Save" is for commitment points, not survival. — [Apple HIG state restoration; NN/g; Material] — [mechanical]
159. **Restore, on return, to where the user was — screen, scroll, input, in-progress flow.** OS-level app restoration plus in-app state restoration; reopening to a cold home screen after a 30-second interruption is a defect. — [Apple HIG; Android state guidance] — [mechanical]
160. **If restoration is stale or ambiguous, offer resumption rather than forcing it.** After days away, "Continue your Tuesday workout? / Start fresh" beats silently reopening a stale context the user has moved past. — [NN/g; consensus] — [judgment]
161. **Warn before discarding — exactly when there is something to discard.** Closing/back/cancel with meaningful unsaved input prompts (or better, keeps a draft); the same actions with nothing at stake never prompt. Wolf-crying confirmations train users to click through the one that matters. — [NN/g confirmation dialogs; Apple HIG] — [mechanical]
162. **Long operations survive backgrounding and navigation.** Uploads, exports, and processing continue (or resume) when the user switches away, with progress visible on return and notification on completion where the platform allows. — [Android foreground services / iOS background tasks; Material progress] — [mechanical]
163. **Timed processes show the clock and offer extension.** Session timeouts, reservation holds, OTP expiry: show remaining time before it matters, warn before expiry, and preserve work through it (see §11.118). — [GOV.UK timeout patterns; WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable] — [mechanical]
164. **Interruptions the app itself causes are the app's to repair.** If an update, forced logout, or migration ends a session, the next launch acknowledges it and restores the user's place — the user never pays for the app's housekeeping. — [consensus] — [judgment]
165. **Offline is an interruption, not an error.** Show cached content read-only or queue-and-sync writes where feasible; label staleness; never present a blank error page for a transient connection drop mid-flow. — [Material offline UX; NN/g] — [judgment]

## 17. Modality and overlay containers

166. **Choose the container by task weight: popover/menu for a one-glance choice, dialog for one focused decision, bottom sheet for mobile option sets and short sub-tasks, side sheet/panel for desktop supplementary context, full screen with a real URL for anything multi-step or resumable.** The heavier and longer the task, the closer to a full page it belongs (see §8.93); the canon's dialog mechanics (canon 01 §K.93) say how — this rule says which. — [Apple HIG modality, Material 3 dialogs and sheets, NN/g modal usage] — [judgment]
167. **Use blocking modality only when the task must be completed or abandoned before anything else makes sense.** Everything else is nonmodal — a sheet the user can peek under, a panel beside the content. Reaching for a modal because it's convenient to build is the top modality defect. — [Apple HIG modality, NN/g overuse of modals] — [judgment]
168. **Bottom sheets follow the platform contract: visible drag handle, detents (partial → full) when content grows, drag-down and scrim-tap dismissal, and inner scrolling only at full height.** A half-open sheet that both scrolls internally and drags is gesture soup; the handle is the signal that it drags. — [Material 3 bottom sheets, Apple HIG sheets/detents] — [mechanical]
169. **Any surface that dismisses by gesture also dismisses by visible control, and dismissal that would lose work is guarded.** Scrim-tap/drag-down for cheap surfaces, an explicit close/Cancel always present, dirty-content interception per §4.44. — [Apple HIG, Material 3, §2.30] — [mechanical]
170. **Never stack transient surfaces more than one deep.** A dialog opening a dialog, or a sheet over a sheet, signals the flow should have been a page or a step sequence; one temporary layer at a time. — [Apple HIG, NN/g] — [judgment]
171. **The same task uses the same container at the same breakpoint everywhere, and containers adapt across widths as a designed pair.** Bottom sheet on phones ↔ centered dialog or side panel on desktop as one deliberate mapping, not two ad-hoc surfaces. — [Material 3 adaptive guidance, DS consensus] — [mechanical]

## 18. Scheduling and calendar views

172. **Schedule views default to the granularity of the user's planning — week view for weekly routines, month for long-range browsing, agenda/list for "what's next" — and the chosen view persists.** — [Google/Apple calendar convergence, NN/g] — [judgment]
173. **Today is always visibly marked and one tap away.** The current day is highlighted in every calendar view, and a "Today" affordance returns from any scrolled position. — [Google/Apple calendar convergence] — [mechanical]
174. **Scheduled items show their state at a glance — upcoming, done, missed/skipped — without color as the only cue.** A plan view where completed and missed look identical fails its one job. — [canon 05 §9.65 (not color alone); fitness-planner convergence] — [mechanical]
175. **Rescheduling is direct: drag-to-move wherever a time grid exists, always with an explicit "move to…" alternative, and recurring items ask edit scope (canon 01 §T.167).** — [Google Calendar convention; WCAG 2.5.7 via canon 01 §M.123] — [mechanical]

## 19. Transactional email as a product surface

176. **Transactional email (verify, reset, receipt, security alert) is a designed product surface with one job and one CTA.** Single column, the action as one prominent button, everything else subordinate; the reset email is a step of the reset flow, not marketing collateral. — [transactional-email consensus (Postmark-class guidance), NN/g email research] — [mechanical]
177. **Subject lines state the event specifically, and the sender name is the product's, consistent across all mail.** "Reset your Chad password" from "Chad" — never "Action required!" from "noreply-svc-7"; users triage by sender + subject alone. — [NN/g email usability, deliverability consensus] — [mechanical]
178. **Emails survive hostile clients: real text content, a working plain-text part, no image-only layouts, buttons that are real links.** Many clients block images by default; meaning must never live only in an image. — [email-engineering consensus, accessibility canon] — [mechanical]
179. **Say why the user got this email and what happens if they ignore it.** Especially for security and verification: "You requested a password reset. If this wasn't you, ignore this — your password is unchanged." — [Google/Apple security-email convention, NN/g] — [mechanical]
180. **Time-limited links state their expiry, and the expired-link landing page offers a fresh one.** A dead reset link with no recovery is a canonical dead end (see §11.115). — [§11.115, GOV.UK] — [mechanical]
181. **Marketing and transactional mail are separate streams: separate consent, separate unsubscribe scope, and unsubscribing from marketing never severs transactional messages.** One-click unsubscribe per §14.149; receipts and security alerts continue regardless. — [CAN-SPAM/GDPR practice, email consensus] — [mechanical]

## 20. In-app notification inbox

182. **If the product has an in-app notification center, it is the durable record of the transient channels: everything pushed is findable there.** Toasts and pushes expire; the inbox doesn't — users who missed the moment can recover it (the same logic as canon 03 §5.37). — [platform convergence (bell-icon inboxes), canon 03 §5.37] — [mechanical]
183. **Unread state is per-item, visually distinct, drives the bell badge, and clears on view — with mark-all-read one tap away.** Badge and inbox must agree (canon 03 §16.120). — [platform convergence, Material badge] — [mechanical]
184. **Each inbox item deep-links to its subject and reads as one scannable line: actor/event, object, relative time — with repetitive events grouped ("3 comments on your template").** — [notification-inbox convergence (GitHub/Slack-class), §14.146] — [mechanical]
185. **The inbox is not a marketing surface, and it doesn't accumulate forever.** Announcements get their own labeled section if they must exist; items age out on a stated or evident retention window. A junk-filled inbox trains abandonment of the whole channel. — [canon 03 §16.119, NN/g notification restraint] — [judgment]

## 21. Guest-to-account migration

186. **Everything a guest creates survives account creation — merged automatically, never discarded.** The upgrade moment is a data handoff: local/anonymous data attaches to the new account without loss. "Sign up and lose your workouts" is a canonical betrayal. — [growth.design onboarding consensus; anonymous-auth link-on-upgrade patterns (Firebase-class)] — [mechanical]
187. **Say what will happen to guest data at the signup moment.** One line — "Your 3 logged workouts will be saved to your account" — turns anxiety into a signup motivator. — [NN/g, growth.design] — [mechanical]
188. **Signing into an existing account from a guest session with local data reconciles safely — merge, or ask — never silently overwriting either side.** The sync-conflict rule (§15.152) applies at the guest boundary too. — [sync consensus applied] — [mechanical]
189. **Guest limits are stated where they bind, before work is invested.** If guest data is device-only or capped, say so at creation time ("Saved on this phone — create an account to back up"), not at the moment of loss. — [trust consensus (canon 06 §P), growth.design] — [mechanical]

## Contested / no-consensus

- **Hard numeric depth/click limits** ("3-click rule", "max 2 levels"): consensus rejects fixed numbers in favor of scent-per-step; any specific ceiling is [contested].
- **Onboarding checklists/gamified progress bars**: strong advocacy in PLG circles (growth.design, Appcues), skepticism from NN/g about checklist fatigue; use is a product call. [contested]
- **Push vs. replace for every URL-state change**: the filters-replace/navigation-push split (§5.55) is majority practice but edge cases (sort? page?) are argued both ways. [contested]
- **Login-wall-free trial depth**: how much value to give before requiring signup is strategy, not consensus — only "don't gate the very first look without reason" is agreed. [contested]
- **One-thing-per-page everywhere**: GOV.UK's strong form is contested outside government/high-stakes contexts; commercial checkouts often perform better with grouped steps. Consensus core is "focused steps," not "one field per screen." [contested]
- **A single retention offer on cancel** (§12.125): regulators in some jurisdictions read *any* interposed save screen as friction; the one-honest-offer position is the design-community norm, not a legal safe harbor. [contested]
- **Mega-menus vs. multiple levels on desktop web**: NN/g endorses well-designed mega-menus; others report scanning overload; genuinely context-dependent. [contested]
- **Adaptive/personalized navigation** (reordering by usage): universally rejected for *primary* nav (§2.25), but personalized shortcuts/recents *sections* are widely endorsed — the boundary is fuzzy. [contested]
- **Infinite scroll vs. pagination vs. Load More for content lists**: RESOLVED — house rule adopted 2026-07-22. Default to Load More with lazy loading (the Baymard-evidenced winner), matching canon 08 §B3.64; infinite scroll only for leisure feeds, numbered pagination only for reference/lookup contexts (08 §B3.65–66). No longer treated as open in this canon.
